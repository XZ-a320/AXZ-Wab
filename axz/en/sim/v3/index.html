Simulator 3.0 (preview)

Air Xiao Ze (AXZ) · Simulator

Fly the two Air Xiao Ze routes in your browser. The aircraft are dimensioned from the same table as the fleet drawings, and the routes, runway headings and cruise levels are the ones already published here. The aerodynamics are coefficient based: lift varies with angle of attack, the wing stalls, the engines take time to spool, the landing gear is a spring and damper, and how gently you arrive depends on how you flare.

3.0 changes the picture, not the aeroplane. Airframes, terrain and airports move to real 3D models, real elevation and real imagery, and the flight model is the one 2.0 flies. 1.0 and 2.0 stay exactly as they are.

    What the assist does

    A keyboard has no analogue axis and does not spring back to centre, so a layer of assist is on by default: it damps pitch and roll, works the rudder to take out sideslip, and rolls slowly level when you let go. Press N to switch it off. With it off you get the bare aerodynamics, which flies more like the real thing and is harder.

    Aircraft you can fly

    The first four are Air Xiao Ze’s own aircraft. The last eleven are not: they are additional types the simulator offers, and they have nothing to do with this airline. Dimensions, wing area, typical operating weight and thrust are all the manufacturer’s published figures. The lift-curve slope is not typed in; it is computed from the span and the wing area through lifting-line theory, so a delta of aspect ratio 1.83 gets 2.9 per radian and an airliner wing of 10.3 gets 5.2, from one formula. Stall and approach speeds follow from that: 158 kt for Concorde, 152 for the F-16, 49 for the Cessna, each within a few knots of the published figure. The same equations fly the 250-tonne 747 and the one-tonne Cessna. The only branches are the three real differences between engines: a propeller, a turbofan, and a low-bypass or turbojet core with a burner behind it. How each one feels is computed too, not tuned. Roll authority is solved backwards from the published maximum roll rate: 15 degrees a second for Concorde, 35 for the 737, 324 for the F-16. The speed it is solved at is the manoeuvring speed, which is the clean stall speed times the root of the certification limit load factor, and that factor is a real number per category: 2.5 for transport, 3.8 for a normal category single, 9 for a fighter. This used to scale inversely with wingspan, so Concorde, whose span is shorter than a 737, was handed more roll authority than a narrowbody and flew like a fighter. It now flies like the 111-tonne delta it is.

    Flying it on a computer

    Press Z, or the Fullscreen button, and the picture fills the screen with the instruments scaled up to match. Z or Esc brings you back. In the page the simulator is about five hundred pixels tall, the flight display takes a third of that, and a runway six miles out is four pixels wide, so there is nothing to see on final. Fullscreen fixes all three. Since the readout below the canvas is out of reach in fullscreen, the flight number, registration and camera are printed in the two lower corners of the panel instead, and the height above the ground appears under the attitude ball once you are below 2,500 ft.

    The cockpit view

    Press C for the cockpit view. There is now a glareshield, window posts and a panel with the flight display mounted in it rather than floating in the air. The eye height is worked out per type from the fuselage diameter and the length, so the flight deck of a 747 is a dozen metres above a Cessna and the horizon crosses the window frame differently when the two rotate. The frame is drawn rather than modelled, and it hides nothing you would have been able to see out of the real one anyway.

    Setting the weather yourself

    It starts calm. Under the starting position are time of day, wind direction, wind speed and turbulence. These four change real quantities inside the simulator rather than a filter over the picture. Time moves the sun, so the shadows swing round with it, night is genuinely dark, and that is the point at which the runway lights start earning their place. Wind direction and speed go straight into the aerodynamics: a headwind changes the ground speed without touching the airspeed, and a crosswind you do not correct for will carry you off the centreline. Turbulence sets the gust amplitude, and on Severe you will be correcting the whole way down final. The wind still slows near the surface, so the crosswind in the flare is always a little less than the one you had on the approach.

    Flying it on a phone

    Phone mode goes fullscreen, locks to landscape, and flies on the gyroscope: tilt the phone forward and back for pitch, rotate it left and right for roll. Neutral is however you were holding it when you pressed the button, so hold it comfortably, and press Recentre whenever you want to reset that. Throttle is the vertical strip on the left, rudder is the horizontal one along the bottom and springs back to centre, and the buttons on the right are gear, flaps, spoilers, brakes and view. iOS asks once for motion permission; decline it and everything still works except the tilt.

    The runways

    Each of the four fields now has the runways it really has rather than one of them. KSFO has the parallel 28L and 28R at their real 228 m spacing plus the crossing 01L and 01R; Salinas has 31 and 26 crossing each other; Pudong gets three of its four parallels; Lukou has 06 and the 07 that opened in 2020. That is not only a picture: a crosswind you cannot hold on 28R is straight down the runway on 01L, and two strips at the same airport ninety degrees apart make the choice a real one. The landing score and the PAPI still read the published runway.

    Reheat

    Concorde and the F-16 have a burner; the other nine do not. It lights past 92 per cent of lever travel, the way a real throttle goes past a detent, and the extra thrust is the manufacturer’s published wet figure rather than the dry one times a factor: the Olympus 593 gives 139.4 kN dry and 169.2 kN lit, the F110 gives 76.3 and 131. The N1 readout stops showing a percentage and shows A/B instead, and the sound gains a layer. Fuel is not modelled here, so nothing punishes you for leaving it in. A turbojet also gains thrust with speed, because the intake is doing work the compressor would otherwise have to, which is exactly why Concorde used the burner to get through the transonic and then cruised supersonically on dry power. The transonic is real: wave drag starts climbing above Mach 0.93, and if you cannot push through it you cannot push through it.

    Fifth generation, and the bombers

    What separates the F-22 from the F-16 is not that it is newer. It supercruises: it holds Mach 1.82 with the burner unlit, the only one of the fifteen that can stay supersonic without reheat, and there is a build gate watching that specifically, because the day it stops being true it has stopped being an F-22. The F-35 has one engine, but that one gives 125 kN dry and 191 kN lit, and its wing is sized for carrying rather than turning, so it accelerates hard and is the slower of the two flat out. Both have twin fins canted outward, which is not styling: a vertical surface returns a radar signal straight back to whatever painted it, and tilting it sends that return somewhere else. The chine along the forebody does two jobs the same way, holding the vortex at high alpha and reflecting sideways rather than forward. The B-2 has no fin and no fuselage at all: the whole aeroplane is one cranked double-W wing with the intakes on TOP, because nothing on a stealth bomber may look down at a radar. It holds its heading with split drag rudders at the tips and rolls like a barn door, 20 degrees a second. The B-52 carries eight engines in four twin pods on a wing flexible enough that its tips move four metres, and at 15 degrees a second it is the slowest-rolling thing here. How far thrust falls away with speed is set by bypass ratio: a CFM56 at 5.5 throws a large mass of air slowly and runs out of use once the aeroplane is fast, while the F118 in a B-2 and the TF33 in a B-52 are both under 1 and barely notice. On the airliner lapse the B-2 could not hold its own cruise. It needed 90 kN and had 63.

    The sound barrier

    Concorde and the F-16 can pass Mach 1 and the other nine cannot, which is not a setting but what the thrust and the wave drag work out to. Three things happen on the way through, and each of them is real. The lift-curve slope rises and then collapses: Prandtl-Glauert makes the wing more sensitive as 1/sqrt(1-M squared), the shocks form and it falls away, and supersonically it settles at the thin-aerofoil value of 4/sqrt(M squared -1), except that a low-aspect-ratio wing cannot exceed its own slender-body limit of pi times AR over two. At their own cruise speeds that puts Concorde, at an aspect ratio of 1.83, down from 2.92 to 2.28, a fifth, while the F-16 at Mach 2 is down from 3.95 to 2.23, which is nearly half. The delta was shaped to hold its lift across the barrier and it does; the fighter was not and does not. That is the reason the two feel so unlike each other at speed. It comes out of the aspect ratio; none of it is typed in. The aerodynamic centre runs aft, so the lift the wing already has starts pushing the nose down. That is Mach tuck, and it is what Concorde pumped twenty tonnes of fuel aft to trim out. Control effectiveness follows the same slope, so the controls go heavy. What you can see: between Mach 0.94 and 1.08 a collar of condensation stands on the rear fuselage, because the pressure drop behind the shock cools the air past its dew point, which is why it is strongest low down in moist air and almost absent high up, and why every famous photograph of one was taken near the sea. In reheat above Mach 1 a row of shock diamonds stands in the exhaust. The sound is a double crack, not a bang: the bow shock arrives, the pressure drops below ambient behind it, and the tail shock brings it back, which the ear hears as two reports about a tenth of a second apart. From the flight deck you barely hear your own, because the shock cone trails behind you and you are in front of it. Going supersonic the cockpit gets quieter, which is the thing every Concorde passenger remembers and no simulator ever models.

    What happens if you crash

    There are three ways to break it, all read off the physics: touching down above 1,200 ft/min, which is about where a 737's gear design limit sits; touching down banked more than 32 degrees; or landing with the gear up. After that the wing keeps a tenth of its lift, the engines stop, the controls do nothing, and the airframe tumbles down and burns where it lands. Press R to start again.

    Sound

    The engine, the airflow, the wheels and the touchdown are synthesised on the spot, not sampled. The four kinds of engine make their noise in different places. A high-bypass turbofan is mostly the fan: a blade-passing whine over a soft exhaust, and the bigger the fan the lower the whine sits. Concorde’s Olympus has no bypass air shielding it, so the exhaust itself carries the sound, raw and wide and edged, with a low rip underneath once the reheat is lit. The F-16’s F110 sits between them, with a harder compressor tone and the same burner. The Cessna is not a turbine at all: it is a four-cylinder firing pulse at 2,400 rpm plus the slap of a two-blade propeller. Four engines are not one engine four times as loud; they are one engine plus a beat, because they never turn at exactly the same speed. All of it tracks N1, so the sound spools when the thrust spools, which a looped sample cannot do. Airflow follows indicated airspeed and gets louder with the gear, flaps or spoilers out; wheel rumble follows ground speed; the thud at touchdown is scaled by the vertical speed. The alerts are the exception: five audio files, 48 KB in total. A Boeing low-speed warning is a recorded human voice saying AIRSPEED LOW, and a synthesised imitation of one particular voice is not synthesis, it is a bad impression. Which set an aeroplane gets follows the aeroplane: the Boeings get the Boeing calls, the Airbuses and Concorde get the Airbus stall warning and its cricket, and the Gulfstream is on the same Honeywell terrain box as the Boeings so it says the same words. A Cessna’s stall warning is a pneumatic reed horn in the wing leading edge and the F-16 has a voice of its own, so those two keep the synthesised tone rather than borrowing an airliner’s throat. The audio downloads on the same press as the engine, and starts muted.

    The height callouts

    Somebody counts you down on final: two thousand five hundred, a thousand, five hundred, four hundred, three hundred, two hundred, one hundred, then seventy, sixty, fifty, forty, thirty, twenty, ten and five. These are seventeen recordings rather than synthesis, for the same reason the alerts are: the calls are a particular human voice, and an approximation of a particular voice is an approximation of nothing. They fire on the CROSSING rather than on the height, and re-arm only after climbing back above it, so a float in the flare cannot shout "twenty" at you four times running. Coming down fast, fifty, forty, thirty and twenty arrive inside a second, which is what a real one does. The files were re-encoded: mono at 32 kbps, silence trimmed off both ends, loudness matched to -16 LUFS. Seventeen calls come to 73 KB, a quarter of what arrived, and nothing about them sounds different. Like the engines, not one byte of it downloads before Start is pressed.

    Failures

    The failure rate setting decides how often the aeroplane breaks by itself. On None it never does, and the only way to break one is to fly it badly, which is where this started. Above that it is a Poisson process: the probability in any step is the rate times the step, and High is roughly one failure an hour, which is far worse than any real aeroplane and is exactly the point. What breaks is weighted by how much of the aeroplane each system is: engines, hydraulics, electrics, gear, flaps, pitot. Every one of them changes something you can feel. An engine out costs thrust and adds a yawing moment you have to hold off with rudder. Losing one of four costs a quarter; losing the only one you have makes a glider; and an outboard engine is harder to hold than an inboard because the arm is longer. With the hydraulics gone the gear and flaps stay exactly where they were, which is nothing on the ground and is the flight you remember if it happens clean on approach. A blocked pitot does not read zero, it freezes at the last thing it saw: the aerodynamics still run on the real speed and only the gauge is wrong, which is the classic failure and the classic killer. Overstress and overspeed break things too. Past the limit load factor damage accumulates, past 1.5 times it the airframe comes apart, and twelve seconds above Vne costs the hydraulics.

    Reading the PAPI

    The four lights to the left of the runway are the visual approach slope indicator. Four white means you are high, four red means you are low, and two of each means you are on the three-degree slope. The angles are the real ones: 2.5, 2.833, 3.167 and 3.5 degrees. The PAPI row in the flight data spells the current state out as W and R.

    Wind

    There is a wind, and it gusts. The wind row gives the direction it blows from and its speed in knots, then H or T for the headwind or tailwind component on the destination runway. The wind slows and backs near the surface, so the crosswind in the flare is not the crosswind on final. The aerodynamics run on the relative airflow rather than the ground track: a headwind lowers your ground speed without touching your airspeed, and a crosswind will drift you off the centreline if you do not crab into it.

    Everything you can see has a reason

    Tyre smoke is released on the touchdown frame and scaled by the vertical speed, so how much smoke you see is how hard the arrival was about to be graded. Wingtip vapour appears only when the wing is near its lift limit, which is high load factor or high angle of attack, the same condition under which a real one condenses its vortex cores. Engine exhaust needs both thrust and dense air to show; contrails need 7,200 m. Clouds, trees and the shadow are hashed from coordinates, so the same place always has the same cloud.

    How the landing is scored

    Touchdown vertical speed is graded against the table on the dispatch desk, using the same bands and the same wording. Landing at KSNS also reports how far off the centreline you were, in metres.
